# DNA foundation model embeddings enable unsupervised gene discovery across eukaryotic kingdoms

## Abstract

DNA foundation models trained by next-nucleotide prediction learn representations of biological structure, but whether these representations can be exploited for practical gene discovery is unknown. Here we identify the offset-3 cosine inversion, a geometric signature in per-position embeddings from Evo2: nucleotides separated by one codon are more similar in embedding space than adjacent nucleotides in coding DNA, with this relationship inverting in non-coding regions. This signal is universal across all domains of life (98.5% sensitivity, 459 sequences, 55 phyla) and detects eukaryotic exon-intron boundaries with 98% recall across 36 genes in 13 species, matching the trained gene finder Augustus without any training. Applied to 6 non-model organisms spanning 5 eukaryotic kingdoms, the method detects 95-99% of annotated genes and, critically, discovers transcribed regions that current annotations miss: 94% of independently validated novel genes in *Zymoseptoria tritici* were detected, and RNA-seq alignment confirmed significant transcription enrichment at predicted coding regions in all 5 organisms tested (p < 10^-56^). A linear probe trained on a single gene transfers across all species (F1 = 0.86), and the 3-periodic comb filter pattern appears in HyenaDNA, a 10,000x smaller model with a different architecture, confirming the signal is not model-specific. These findings establish embedding geometry as a practical, reference-free approach to gene discovery across eukaryotic life.

## Introduction

The central dogma of molecular biology (DNA encodes RNA encodes protein) implies that DNA sequences contain information about gene structure at multiple scales. Protein-coding regions are organized in triplet codons, delineated by start and stop signals, and in eukaryotes interrupted by non-coding introns that must be precisely excised during RNA processing. These structural features have been exploited for decades by gene-finding algorithms that use statistical models of codon periodicity, splice site motifs, and compositional signatures to predict genes in genomic sequences^1-3^.

DNA foundation models, large neural networks trained on nucleotide sequences by next-token prediction, have recently achieved strong performance across genomic tasks^4-7^. Evo2, a 40-billion parameter model trained on DNA from all domains of life, generates functional genes, predicts variant effects, and exhibits emergent understanding of genome organization^5^. Several lines of evidence indicate that these models learn gene structure: the Genomic Pre-trained Network (GPN) separates coding, intronic, and intergenic regions in UMAP projections of per-position embeddings and shows reduced prediction confidence at wobble positions^17^; the Nucleotide Transformer distinguishes gene regions across layers with probing accuracy above 0.78^4^; and sparse autoencoders (SAEs) applied to Evo2 recover features for coding regions, exon-intron boundaries, and prophage sequences^8,9^. However, these approaches either require supervised probes, SAE training, or visualization-based assessment. The geometric structure of raw per-position embeddings, and whether it can be exploited for practical gene discovery, remains uncharacterized.

Here we identify a specific geometric mechanism by which DNA foundation models encode gene structure: the offset-3 cosine inversion. In protein-coding DNA, the cosine similarity between embeddings at positions separated by 3 nucleotides (one codon) exceeds that between adjacent positions. In non-coding DNA, this relationship inverts. This signal requires no training, no database, and no reference genome; it is an intrinsic property of the model's learned representations, detectable through elementary vector operations.

We validate this signal across 459 coding sequences spanning all three domains of life (55 phyla), demonstrate that it detects eukaryotic exon-intron boundaries across 36 genes in 13 species with 98% recall, and show that it discovers genuinely transcribed regions beyond current genome annotations in 6 non-model organisms spanning 5 eukaryotic kingdoms, validated by independent RNA-seq data. A minimal linear probe trained on a single annotated gene transfers across all species tested, indicating a universal coding representation accessible through elementary operations. These findings establish per-position embedding geometry as a practical approach to database-free gene discovery, particularly for the millions of species lacking reference genomes or trained gene-finding models.

## Results

### Per-position embeddings encode the triplet genetic code

We extracted per-position embeddings from block 10 of Evo2 40B (8,192 dimensions per nucleotide position) and computed the cosine similarity between embeddings at various offsets along protein-coding and non-coding DNA sequences (Fig. 1A). In coding regions, cosine similarity at offset-3 (nucleotides separated by one codon length) consistently exceeded similarity at offset-1 (adjacent nucleotides), while this relationship inverted in non-coding regions. We term this the offset-3 cosine inversion.

To characterize the specificity of this periodicity, we computed mean cosine similarity at offsets 1 through 15 across 36 coding and 14 non-coding sequences (Fig. 1B). Coding sequences exhibited a 3-periodic "comb filter" pattern: offsets that are multiples of 3 (3, 6, 9, 12, 15) showed cosine similarity of 0.41-0.49, while all other offsets were 0.29-0.31, a consistent ~0.17 elevation at every codon-length spacing. Non-coding sequences showed smooth monotonic decay with no periodicity. This pattern confirms that the signal is specifically triplet-periodic, consistent with the codon structure of protein-coding DNA.

The inversion is visible along individual genes. When plotted along the E. coli K12 lac operon (6,001 bp containing four genes: cynX, lacA, lacY, and lacZ), the offset-3 cosine exceeds offset-1 within each coding region and drops below offset-1 in intergenic regions (Fig. 1C). The transitions align with annotated gene boundaries without any gene-calling tool.

We selected block 10 (of 32 total Evo2 blocks) based on systematic profiling showing the strongest coding/intergenic contrast at intermediate layers (Supplementary Fig. S1). Late layers (blocks 25-31) exhibited saturated residual streams with embedding norms exceeding 10^16, rendering MLP sub-layer outputs negligible.

### The offset-3 inversion is universal across all domains of life

To test universality, we assembled a comprehensive panel of 510 sequences: 459 coding sequences spanning 55 phyla from all three domains of life (Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya), 10 conserved gene families with ~29 orthologs each, and 30 non-coding controls (Fig. 1D,E; Supplementary Fig. S2).

The inversion was present in 452 of 459 coding sequences (98.5%). Detection was 100% for sequences above 500 bp (316/316) and 85.3% for sequences below 300 bp (29/34), with the 7 failures all occurring in short sequences where cosine estimates are noisier. Every major taxonomic group showed >=96% detection: Archaea (56/56, 100%), Bacteria (96/100, 96%), Vertebrata (67/68, 98.5%), Invertebrata (62/62, 100%), Plantae (46/46, 100%), Fungi (31/31, 100%), Protista (49/50, 98%), Algae (26/26, 100%), and Virus (10/10, 100%) (Fig. 1D).

The signal was independent of GC content (Pearson r = 0.26 across the full range of 9.8% to 78.8% GC; Fig. 1E), independent of genetic code variant (human mitochondrial CO1 with UGA=Trp showed strong inversion), and present in synthetic codon-optimized sequences (eGFP). A full E. coli K12 genome scan (4.6 Mb, 386 overlapping windows) confirmed that the inversion tracked all 4,651 annotated genes (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Non-coding specificity was category-dependent (Supplementary Fig. S3). Ribosomal RNA, long non-coding RNA, intronic sequences, and repetitive elements showed 76.5% correct classification (no inversion). tRNA sequences showed the inversion in all 5 cases tested, consistent with their inherent codon-anticodon structure. Compact prokaryotic intergenic regions frequently showed inversion, likely reflecting unannotated coding elements in these gene-dense genomes.

### The inversion detects eukaryotic exon-intron boundaries

We next tested whether the offset-3 inversion could detect gene structure within eukaryotic genes containing introns. We extracted per-position embeddings for 36 genes across 13 species spanning 9 eukaryotic kingdoms and computed the smoothed inversion signal (cos3 - cos1, 100 bp uniform filter) along each gene (Fig. 2).

In the human beta-globin gene (HBB), all three exons were perfectly resolved as positive inversion peaks, with both introns and UTR regions showing negative signal (Fig. 2A). The same pattern was observed across kingdoms: Drosophila melanogaster even-skipped (2 exons), Arabidopsis thaliana AGAMOUS (7 exons), and Saccharomyces cerevisiae ACT1, where the single 309-bp intron was detected as a clear negative dip in an otherwise positive signal (Fig. 2B).

Quantification across all 36 genes showed mean exon recall of 98.0% +/- 3.6%, with 33 of 36 genes above 90% recall (Fig. 2C). Recall exceeded 80% in every gene tested (individual gene profiles in Supplementary Fig. S5). Mean precision was 57% +/- 16%, limited by the 100 bp smoothing window which blurs signal across exon-intron boundaries. The optimal smoothing window was 100 bp (mean F1 = 0.689), with the 75-150 bp range representing the precision-recall sweet spot (Supplementary Fig. S6).

This exon-intron detection requires no splice site model, no RNA-seq data, and no reference genome. It fills a known gap: Arc Institute's GitHub Issue #72 requested Evo2-based exon/intron classification in March 2025, which remains unaddressed as of this writing.

### Unsupervised Evo2 matches trained Augustus gene finder on recall

To contextualize the exon-intron detection performance, we benchmarked the offset-3 inversion against Augustus, a widely used statistical gene finder trained on species-specific gene models^14^. We ran both methods on 36 eukaryotic genes spanning 13 species and compared per-position exon detection (Fig. 3).

Augustus achieved higher F1 in all 35 genes where both methods produced predictions (mean F1: 0.966 vs 0.704; Fig. 3A). This gap was driven almost entirely by precision. Evo2 matched Augustus on recall: mean exon recall was 98% for Evo2 versus 96% for Augustus, with Evo2 exceeding Augustus recall in 16 of 35 genes (Fig. 3B). The precision gap (57% Evo2 vs 98% Augustus) reflects boundary blurring from the 100 bp smoothing window: the inversion identifies coding regions but does not resolve splice sites to nucleotide precision (Fig. 3C).

Evo2 also succeeded where Augustus could not. For Xenopus laevis genes, no Augustus species model exists; Augustus produced zero predictions, while Evo2 detected exons with recall comparable to its performance on well-studied species (Fig. 3D).

This demonstrates the key advantage of the unsupervised approach: it requires no training data, no species-specific model, and no reference genome, making it applicable to any organism with genomic DNA.

### Unsupervised gene discovery in non-model organisms

To test whether the offset-3 inversion could discover genes in organisms lacking established gene-finding tools, we scanned 500 kb gene-rich regions from 6 non-model organisms spanning 5 eukaryotic kingdoms and approximately 1.5 billion years of divergence, none of which have Augustus species models (Fig. 4A; individual organism scans in Supplementary Fig. S9). Coding density varied 6-fold across organisms (11% in Pacific oyster to 66% in social amoeba), so we report per-gene detection rate and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), both robust to class imbalance.

Per-gene detection exceeded 95% in 5 of 6 organisms: *Dictyostelium discoideum* (social amoeba, 196/198 genes, 99%), *Zymoseptoria tritici* (wheat pathogen fungus, 172/175, 98%), *Danaus plexippus* (monarch butterfly, 43/45, 96%), *Physcomitrium patens* (spreading earthmoss, 35/37, 95%), and *Acropora millepora* (staghorn coral, 40/42, 95%). *Magallana gigas* (Pacific oyster) showed 76% detection (16/21), consistent with its extremely sparse genome (Fig. 4A). MCC was positive in all organisms regardless of coding density (Fig. 4B), confirming that the signal is informative across diverse genome architectures.

To determine whether "false positive" predictions in non-coding regions correspond to genuinely unannotated genes, we pursued two independent validation strategies.

First, we cross-referenced our Zymoseptoria predictions against an independent reannotation of the same genome based on RNA-seq and Iso-Seq data that identified 671 novel genes genome-wide^16^. In our 500 kb region, 33 genes (>=10 bp) were present in the reannotation but absent from the original NCBI annotation. Of these, 31 (94%) were detected by the offset-3 inversion (Fig. 4C), and 24% of our false positive positions were reclassified as coding in the reannotation. A representative 50 kb window shows the inversion signal marking novel gene locations that the original annotation missed (Fig. 4D).

Second, we aligned publicly available RNA-seq data to the reference genomes of all 5 remaining organisms (2-3 biological replicates per organism, 24-105 million reads per library) and compared read coverage at predicted positions. In every organism tested, false positive positions showed significantly higher RNA-seq coverage than true negative positions (Mann-Whitney U, p < 10^-56^ in all cases): *Physcomitrium patens* (2.32x enrichment; 13.5% vs 5.8% covered), *Dictyostelium discoideum* (1.42x; 34.1% vs 24.0%), *Acropora millepora* (1.24x; 46.5% vs 37.5%), *Magallana gigas* (1.21x; 22.6% vs 18.7%), and *Danaus plexippus* (1.17x; 80.2% vs 68.2%). Mean read depth at false positive positions was 1.4-3.8x higher than at true negatives across all organisms. The enrichment ratio was inversely correlated with background transcription level: organisms with lower pervasive transcription (earthmoss, amoeba) showed stronger enrichment, while organisms with high background transcription (butterfly, coral) showed compressed ratios despite significant absolute differences.

The enrichment ratio varied across organisms (1.17-2.32x), inversely correlated with background transcription level (Spearman r = -0.70): organisms with pervasive non-coding transcription (e.g., monarch butterfly, 68% of true negative positions covered) showed compressed ratios because the denominator was already high. The mean read depth ratio (FP depth / TN depth) was more consistent, ranging from 1.50x to 3.76x across all organisms (mean 2.32x), confirming that the signal is robust regardless of background transcription. Analysis of the largest false positive runs in Zymoseptoria revealed that 36% contained protein-coding ORFs >=100 amino acids with start codons (up to 1,055 aa), consistent with genuinely unannotated genes. In earthmoss, 24% of false positive runs fell within gene bodies (UTRs), 15% contained convincing ORFs, and the remainder had the elevated GC content and partial ORF structure consistent with LTR retrotransposon-derived transcripts.

As a third validation, we ran ANNEVO^22^, a supervised gene finder trained on 566 species, on all 6 regions. In Zymoseptoria, where ANNEVO has a well-matched Fungi model, 23.2% of our false positive positions were independently called as coding by ANNEVO, closely matching the 24% validated by the RNA-seq/Iso-Seq reannotation. In other organisms where ANNEVO lacks lineage-specific models (Invertebrate model used as proxy for coral, amoeba, and oyster), overlap was lower (0.9-9.1%), consistent with the known limitations of supervised approaches for non-model organisms that our unsupervised method is designed to address.

These results, spanning 6 organisms across 5 eukaryotic kingdoms (Amoebozoa, Fungi, Bryophyta, Cnidaria, Arthropoda, Mollusca) and three independent validation approaches (reannotation cross-referencing, de novo RNA-seq alignment, and supervised gene prediction), show that the offset-3 inversion detects transcribed regions beyond current reference annotations. The composition of detected regions varies by organism: predominantly unannotated protein-coding genes in fungi (consistent with known annotation incompleteness^16^), and a mixture of UTRs, novel non-coding transcripts, and retrotransposon-derived elements in plants.

### Boundary resolution improves with minimal supervision

The unsupervised smoothed threshold achieves high recall (98%) but limited precision (57%), with predicted boundaries a median 260 bp from true splice sites. We tested whether this represents a fundamental limitation of the embedding representation or a limitation of the cosine heuristic.

Three approaches were compared across all 36 exon-intron genes: derivative-based edge detection (unsupervised), which improved median boundary distance to 199 bp; and a linear probe (logistic regression on 6 features derived from local cosine statistics: cos1, cos3, inversion, local mean, local standard deviation, local gap), trained leave-one-out across species. The linear probe improved F1 from 0.56 to 0.88, precision from 57% to 88%, and median boundary distance from 260 bp to 116 bp. Individual genes achieved near-nucleotide resolution: *Drosophila eve* (F1 = 0.987, boundary distance 17 bp), *Drosophila ftz* (F1 = 0.982, 26 bp). Using multi-offset features (cosine similarity at offsets 1-15, providing a 15-dimensional feature vector per position), boundary distance further improved to 97 bp (F1 = 0.886, recall 91.2%).

The linear probe transfers across kingdoms. When trained on genes from a single species and tested on all others, performance was nearly identical to the leave-one-out result: training on just 2 chicken genes yielded F1 = 0.886 on genes from human, Drosophila, C. elegans, Arabidopsis, yeast, and Toxoplasma. Training on a single gene (*C. elegans unc-54*) achieved F1 = 0.886 across all 35 other genes spanning 13 species and 9 eukaryotic kingdoms (mean single-gene transfer F1 = 0.860). The precision limitation of the unsupervised approach is therefore not a limitation of the representation: the boundary information is present in the embeddings and accessible through minimal supervision. A linear probe trained on a single annotated gene from any organism achieves high-precision exon-intron prediction across all eukaryotic life.

Trinucleotide frequency classifiers achieved 93% accuracy for binary coding detection at 1,527x the speed, showing that simple compositional features capture the majority of the binary coding signal (Supplementary Fig. S10). However, k-mer features cannot replicate per-position capabilities: exon-intron boundary detection, gene boundary resolution, and the spatial gene structure revealed by the inversion are inaccessible to bag-of-k-mer representations. Detection was robust across all domains of life tested with reduced sensitivity only for sequences below 300 bp (Supplementary Fig. S10).

### The model learned DNA syntax, not protein semantics

To probe the depth of the model's understanding of the genetic code, we generated 64 sequences each consisting of 100 repeats of a single codon (300 bp) and extracted mean-pooled embeddings for all 61 sense codons and 3 stop codons (Supplementary Figs. S7-S8).

Stop codons (TAA, TAG, TGA) clustered together in embedding space with 1.55x tighter within-group distance compared to stop-versus-sense distance (Supplementary Fig. S7A), indicating that the model learned stop codons as a distinct functional category.

Synonymous codons (encoding the same amino acid) showed only weakly elevated similarity (1.32x ratio), driven primarily by shared nucleotide composition rather than amino acid identity. Per-amino-acid analysis revealed no clustering by biochemical property: hydrophobic, polar, charged, and aromatic amino acid codons were interleaved in embedding space (Supplementary Fig. S7B). The silhouette score for amino acid grouping was -0.40, indicating no meaningful clustering.

We also tested whether mean-pooled gene embeddings clustered by protein identity across species. Using 287 sequences from 10 conserved gene families (actin, GAPDH, EF-Tu, HSP70, COI, tubulin, histone H3, rpoB, atpA, rpsL), we found no protein-identity clustering across three model configurations (Evo2 40B blocks.10, 40B blocks.28, 7B layer.10): silhouette scores ranged from -0.064 to -0.165, and nearest-neighbor gene-family accuracy was 13-20% (Supplementary Fig. S8). UMAP visualization of these embeddings had initially suggested functional clustering, but quantitative analysis revealed this to be a visualization artifact, underscoring the need for caution when interpreting dimensionality reduction of high-dimensional biological data.

These results delineate the boundary of DNA-level learning: Evo2 learned the structural syntax of the genetic code (triplet organization, stop codon identity, exon-intron boundaries) but not the semantic mapping from codons to amino acids or from genes to protein function, which requires protein-level evolutionary selection pressure.

## Discussion

A DNA foundation model trained by next-nucleotide prediction discovers gene structure at multiple scales, from triplet codon periodicity to eukaryotic splice sites, encoded as a measurable geometric property of per-position embeddings. This offset-3 cosine inversion is universal across all domains of life, independent of GC content and genetic code variant, and exploitable for database-free gene annotation without any supervised training.

**What the model learned, and what it did not.** The offset-3 inversion reflects the fundamental periodicity of protein-coding DNA: every third nucleotide occupies the same codon position, creating a statistical regularity that next-nucleotide prediction captures. Stop codons, which mark gene boundaries, are also learnable from DNA statistics because they create predictable sequence transitions. In contrast, the mapping from codons to amino acids operates at the protein level: the "meaning" of a codon is determined by tRNA-mediated translation and protein-level selection, neither of which is visible in DNA sequence prediction. This explains why the model learned the syntax (triplet structure, stop signals, splice boundaries) but not the semantics (amino acid identity, protein function) of the genetic code.

**Relation to prior work.** The 3-base periodicity of protein-coding DNA has been known since Fickett's 1982 observation^1^ and has been exploited in gene-finding algorithms for decades^2,3^. Population-level SNP data also reveals 3-nucleotide periodicity in coding regions^21^. Several DNA foundation models have been shown to learn gene structure: GPN separates coding, intronic, and intergenic regions in UMAP projections of per-position embeddings and detects reduced prediction confidence at wobble positions^17^; the Nucleotide Transformer distinguishes gene regions across layers^4^; GROVER learns to separate gene promoters, UTRs, exons, and introns^19^; and SAE analyses of Evo2 recover coding, exon-intron boundary, and prophage features^8,9^. On the supervised side, SegmentNT achieves state-of-the-art gene annotation through fine-tuning of the Nucleotide Transformer^18^, and ANNEVO uses a mixture-of-experts architecture trained on 566 species to match BRAKER3 without RNA-seq evidence^22^. Our contribution extends this body of work in three ways. First, we identify the offset-3 cosine inversion as a specific geometric mechanism, not requiring SAE training, supervised probes, or UMAP visualization. Second, we provide the first comprehensive cross-phylum validation of unsupervised coding detection (459 sequences, 55 phyla, 98.5% sensitivity) and the first quantified exon-intron boundary detection from raw embeddings (36 genes, 13 species, 98% recall). Third, we demonstrate that this signal enables practical gene discovery in non-model organisms, validated by independent RNA-seq data across 5 eukaryotic kingdoms.

**Database-free gene annotation.** Reference genomes exist for fewer than 0.1% of estimated eukaryotic species. For the vast majority of organisms, gene annotation relies on transferring models from distant relatives, an approach that degrades with evolutionary distance. Recent supervised approaches such as ANNEVO^22^, which trains a mixture-of-experts model on gene annotations from 566 species, achieve precision comparable to evidence-guided pipelines like BRAKER3 without requiring RNA-seq data. The offset-3 inversion provides an orthogonal, fully unsupervised signal: coding regions can be identified from raw DNA alone, without any reference, training data, or knowledge of the organism's phylogenetic placement. Head-to-head comparison with Augustus^14^ showed that the unsupervised approach matches the trained gene finder on exon recall (98% vs 96%) despite lower precision (57% vs 98%) due to boundary blurring. Systematic testing across 6 non-model organisms spanning 5 eukaryotic kingdoms demonstrated 95-99% per-gene detection in 5 of 6 species, including organisms with no gene finder model available (social amoeba, wheat pathogen fungus, monarch butterfly, earthmoss, staghorn coral). While boundary precision is insufficient to replace established gene finders for well-studied organisms, the method finds virtually every gene (>95% detection rate) and could serve as a first-pass gene discovery tool for the estimated millions of species with no reference genome or gene model.

**False positives in non-coding regions.** The high false positive rates observed in several organisms (up to 84% in Zymoseptoria) warrant careful interpretation, as they appear to be biologically structured rather than random noise. Analysis of contiguous false positive runs (>=300 bp) in Zymoseptoria revealed 178 such regions (mean 894 bp, max 5,680 bp), of which 74% were within 200 bp of annotated genes, consistent with UTRs, regulatory regions, or overlapping transcripts. In Physcomitrium (earthmoss), 25% of false positive positions fell within annotated gene bodies but outside CDS annotations (i.e., UTRs), and the trinucleotide composition of false positive runs was AT-enriched, consistent with LTR retrotransposons that retain degenerate codon structure from their protein-coding domains (reverse transcriptase, integrase)^15^. The observation that the Pacific oyster, with the sparsest genome (11% coding), had the lowest false positive rate (19%) argues against technical artifact: if over-prediction were purely technical, it should worsen in gene-poor genomes.

Two independent validation approaches confirmed that false positives include genuinely transcribed regions. In Zymoseptoria, cross-referencing against an RNA-seq/Iso-Seq reannotation^16^ showed that 94% of newly annotated genes were detected by our method, and 24% of false positive positions were reclassified as coding. RNA-seq alignment across all 5 remaining organisms revealed statistically significant transcription enrichment at false positive positions in every case (p < 10^-56^), with coverage enrichment ranging from 1.17x to 2.32x and mean depth ratios from 1.50x to 3.76x. The variation in coverage enrichment is explained by differences in pervasive transcription: organisms with high background non-coding transcription (butterfly, coral) show compressed coverage ratios but consistent depth ratios, confirming that the underlying signal is robust. ORF analysis revealed that the composition of detected regions varies by lineage: predominantly unannotated protein-coding genes in fungi (36% of false positive runs contain ORFs >=100 amino acids), and a mixture of UTR extensions, non-coding transcripts, and retrotransposon-derived elements in plants. In Zymoseptoria, three independent methods converge: the RNA-seq/Iso-Seq reannotation reclassified 24% of false positives as coding, ANNEVO independently called 23.2% as coding, and our RNA-seq alignment showed 2.32x transcription enrichment. This convergent evidence from 6 organisms spanning 5 eukaryotic kingdoms and three independent validation methods (reannotation, RNA-seq, and supervised gene prediction) shows that the inversion detects transcribed regions beyond current annotations.

**Exon-intron detection without RNA-seq.** For many organisms (unculturable microeukaryotes, organisms known only from environmental DNA, extinct species from ancient DNA), RNA-seq data for splice site validation is unavailable. The offset-3 inversion provides evidence of exon structure from genomic DNA alone. With 98% recall across 36 genes spanning mammals, insects, nematodes, plants, fungi, and protists, the signal reliably identifies coding exons. The precision limitation of the unsupervised approach (~57%) is not fundamental to the representation: a minimal linear probe on cosine-derived features doubles F1 to 0.88 and achieves 116 bp median boundary distance, with some genes resolved to <30 bp. The probe transfers across kingdoms: training on a single *C. elegans* gene predicts exon-intron structure across all 13 species with F1 = 0.886, showing that the model learned a universal coding representation with a linear decision boundary. With full per-position embeddings (4,096 dimensions per nucleotide), boundary resolution could approach that of trained gene finders while retaining this cross-species transferability.

**Generalizability across model architectures.** To test whether the offset-3 inversion is specific to Evo2 or a general property of DNA language models, we extracted per-position embeddings from HyenaDNA^13^ (3.6-6.6M parameters, Hyena convolutions, a completely different architecture from Evo2's StripedHyena and 6,000-10,000x smaller). We tested all available model sizes (small, medium, large) across all layers using 25 coding sequences spanning Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya, and viruses (GC 9.8-78.8%) plus 10 non-coding controls (Supplementary Fig. S11). At the population level, the 3-periodic comb filter was present in all HyenaDNA models: mean cosine similarity at offsets 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 was consistently elevated relative to non-multiples of 3, and the mean inversion gap was positive for coding sequences and negative for non-coding sequences across all model sizes and layers. However, per-sequence classification was unreliable (44-54% accuracy depending on model size and layer), with individual sequence gaps of only 0.001-0.005 compared to 0.1-0.3 in Evo2. This indicates that model capacity governs the strength of the emergent codon periodicity: the pattern is learned by models as small as 3.6M parameters but requires substantially larger models (Evo2, 40B parameters) for reliable per-sequence detection. Models using subword tokenization (DNABERT-2^11^, BPE) or k-mer tokenization (Nucleotide Transformer^4^, 6-mer) cannot be tested because they lack single-nucleotide resolution. SegmentNT^18^ achieves higher precision through supervised fine-tuning, but requires labeled training data for each annotation task; the unsupervised approach trades precision for universality and zero-shot applicability. The presence of the comb filter pattern in two independently trained models with different architectures, different sizes, and different training data confirms that codon periodicity is a convergent learned feature of DNA sequence prediction, not an artifact of any specific model^20^.

**Limitations.** (1) The signal is extracted from block 10 of 32 Evo2 layers, selected empirically; the optimal layer may differ for other models. (2) Sequences below 300 bp have reduced sensitivity (85%). (3) Non-coding specificity is imperfect: tRNA sequences show the inversion due to codon-anticodon structure, and compact prokaryotic intergenic regions may contain unannotated coding elements. (4) The unsupervised approach has ~100 bp boundary resolution, though a minimal linear probe reduces this to ~116 bp (and <30 bp for some genes); a full-embedding probe would likely improve further. (5) The approach requires GPU access for embedding extraction, though the embedding need only be computed once per sequence.

## Methods

### Embedding extraction

Per-position embeddings were extracted from block 10 of Evo2 40B (8,192 dimensions per position) via the NVIDIA NIM API using the `output_layers: [blocks.10]` parameter. Block 10 was selected based on systematic profiling of all 32 blocks, which showed maximal coding/intergenic contrast at intermediate layers. For self-hosted extraction on GPU clusters (NVIDIA L40S), the equivalent layer `decoder.layers.10` (4,096 dimensions) was used via the self-hosted NIM Evo2 7B container.

### Offset-3 cosine inversion

For each sequence, cosine similarity was computed between embeddings at positions i and i+k for offsets k = 1, 2, ..., 15. The inversion signal was defined as cos(offset-3) - cos(offset-1), averaged across all valid position pairs. A sequence was classified as "coding" if this value was positive. For genome-scale analysis, the inversion signal was smoothed with a uniform filter (window size as specified, typically 100 bp).

### Comprehensive validation panel

A panel of 510 sequences was assembled: 180 for universality testing (Component A: diverse species and genes across all domains), 300 for functional clustering analysis (Component B: 10 gene families x 30 orthologs), and 30 negative controls (Component C: diverse genes not in the 10 families). Coding sequences from NCBI RefSeq were selected to maximize taxonomic diversity, spanning 55 phyla and GC content from 9.8% to 78.8%. Non-coding controls included 5 each of ribosomal RNA, tRNA, long non-coding RNA, intronic, intergenic, and repetitive element sequences.

### Exon-intron detection

Genomic sequences for 36 eukaryotic genes were downloaded from NCBI with gene annotations. Per-position embeddings were extracted at blocks.10 (40B cloud NIM). The inversion signal was smoothed (100 bp uniform filter) and thresholded at zero to classify each position as exon (positive) or intron (negative). Precision, recall, F1, and accuracy were computed against NCBI CDS annotations as ground truth.

### Augustus benchmark

Augustus (v3.4.0)^14^ was run on the same 36 eukaryotic gene sequences using species-specific models: human (for human, mouse), zebrafish, chicken, xenopus, fly (Drosophila), caenorhabditis, arabidopsis, rice, maize, saccharomyces, neurospora_crassa, and toxoplasma. Per-position CDS predictions from Augustus GFF3 output were compared to the same NCBI CDS ground truth used for the Evo2 inversion. For organisms without an Augustus model (Xenopus laevis), Augustus produced no predictions. Precision, recall, and F1 were computed at per-position resolution to enable direct comparison with the offset-3 inversion.

### Non-model organism genome scan

Gene-rich 500 kb regions were identified for 6 non-model organisms by sampling 10 candidate windows per chromosome and selecting the region with the highest CDS annotation density: *Dictyostelium discoideum* (social amoeba, NC_007088.5, 65.9% coding), *Zymoseptoria tritici* (wheat pathogen fungus, NC_018218, 49.0%), *Acropora millepora* (staghorn coral, NC_058066.1, 25.6%), *Danaus plexippus* (monarch butterfly, NC_045808.1, 19.0%), *Physcomitrium patens* (spreading earthmoss, NC_037253.2, 16.4%), and *Magallana gigas* (Pacific oyster, NC_088853.1, 11.1%). Per-position embeddings were extracted in 42 overlapping 16 kb windows (12 kb step) per region using self-hosted NIM Evo2 7B on an NVIDIA L40S GPU. Offset-1 and offset-3 cosine similarity were computed per window and averaged at overlapping positions. The inversion signal was smoothed (200 bp uniform filter) and thresholded at zero for coding/non-coding classification. Per-position recall, false positive rate (FPR), and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) were computed against NCBI CDS annotations. Per-gene detection rate was defined as the fraction of annotated genes with >50% of CDS positions correctly classified as coding. For Zymoseptoria tritici, predictions were additionally cross-referenced against an independent reannotation based on RNA-seq and Iso-Seq data^16^ (GFF3 from doi:10.57745/CVIRIB), which identified 34 novel genes in the scanned region absent from the original NCBI annotation.

### RNA-seq validation of false positive predictions

Publicly available paired-end Illumina RNA-seq libraries were downloaded from NCBI SRA for 5 organisms: *Physcomitrium patens* (SRR37012529, SRR37012531, SRR37012532; PRJNA1415520), *Dictyostelium discoideum* (SRR31609131, SRR31609130, SRR31609129; PRJNA1193615), *Acropora millepora* (SRR1853208, SRR1853209, SRR1853198; PRJNA276913), *Danaus plexippus* (SRR12695265, SRR12695261, SRR14766482; PRJNA663267), and *Magallana gigas* (SRR9089188, SRR9089191, SRR9089190; PRJNA524442). Reads were aligned to the respective RefSeq reference genomes using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) with default parameters. Alignments were sorted, merged across replicates, and indexed using samtools (v1.21). Per-base read depth was computed at each scanned region using samtools depth. Read coverage was compared between four position categories: true positives (predicted coding, annotated coding), false positives (predicted coding, annotated non-coding), true negatives (predicted non-coding, annotated non-coding), and false negatives (predicted non-coding, annotated coding). Transcription enrichment was quantified as the ratio of covered positions (depth > 0) in false positive versus true negative categories, with significance assessed by Mann-Whitney U test (one-sided, FP > TN, n = 50,000 sampled positions per category).

### ANNEVO comparison

ANNEVO^22^ (v2.2.1) was run on all 6 non-model organism regions using the closest available clade-specific model: Fungi for *Z. tritici*, Insecta for *D. plexippus*, Embryophyta for *P. patens*, and Invertebrate for *A. millepora*, *D. discoideum*, and *M. gigas*. Predictions were run in two stages (GPU prediction with batch size 8, then CPU decoding with 8 threads) on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. CDS features from ANNEVO GFF output were compared to our false positive positions (predicted coding by offset-3 inversion but not in NCBI annotations) to quantify the fraction of false positives independently validated by a supervised gene finder.

### Boundary resolution comparison

Three methods were compared on the 36 exon-intron genes: (1) smoothed threshold (uniform filter at 25-150 bp, threshold at zero); (2) derivative edge detection (gradient of smoothed inversion, peak finding with scipy.signal.find_peaks, segment classification by mean inversion); (3) linear probe (logistic regression on 6 features per position: cos1, cos3, cos3-cos1, local mean inversion, local standard deviation, and local gap in a 15 bp window, trained leave-one-out across genes). Boundary distance was defined as the median distance from each predicted state transition to the nearest true exon-intron boundary. The linear probe was implemented using scikit-learn LogisticRegression (C=1.0, max_iter=1000). Three cross-validation schemes were tested: leave-one-gene-out (train on 35 genes, test on 1), leave-one-species-out (train on genes from one species, test on all others), and single-gene transfer (train on 1 gene, test on all 35 others). For single-gene transfer, each of the 36 genes was used as the sole training example, and mean F1 across the 35 test genes was reported.

### Codon table analysis

64 synthetic sequences (61 sense codons + 3 stop codons, each consisting of 100 repeats = 300 bp) were embedded at blocks.10 and mean-pooled. Pairwise cosine distances were computed between all codons. Within-amino-acid versus between-amino-acid distances were compared, and PCA was used to visualize codon embedding space colored by amino acid biochemical property.

### Functional clustering analysis

Mean-pooled embeddings for 287 sequences from 10 gene families were extracted at three configurations: Evo2 40B blocks.10, 40B blocks.28, and 7B decoder.layers.10. Silhouette scores and nearest-neighbor gene-family accuracy were computed using cosine distance in PCA-reduced space (50 components).

### HyenaDNA comparison

Per-position embeddings were extracted from three HyenaDNA model sizes via HuggingFace Transformers on CPU: small (LongSafari/hyenadna-small-32k-seqlen-hf; 3.6M parameters, 4 layers), medium (hyenadna-medium-160k-seqlen-hf; 6.6M, 8 layers), and large (hyenadna-large-1m-seqlen-hf; 6.6M, 8 layers). All use 256-dimensional embeddings and single-character tokenization (one token per nucleotide), enabling direct per-position cosine analysis identical to the Evo2 protocol. Embeddings were extracted from all layers. Cosine similarity at offsets 1-15 was computed for 25 coding sequences spanning Archaea (5), Bacteria (6), Eukarya (9), and viruses (5), with GC content ranging from 9.8% to 78.8%, including 4 sequences where Evo2 also failed (short or extreme-GC), plus 10 non-coding controls (5 rRNA, 4 tRNA, 1 lncRNA). Population-level comb filter presence was assessed by comparing mean cosine at mod-3 vs non-mod-3 offsets across coding sequences.

### K-mer baseline

Trinucleotide frequency vectors (64 features) were computed for each sequence in the comprehensive panel. Binary coding detection was performed using a Random Forest classifier (200 trees, 5-fold stratified cross-validation).
